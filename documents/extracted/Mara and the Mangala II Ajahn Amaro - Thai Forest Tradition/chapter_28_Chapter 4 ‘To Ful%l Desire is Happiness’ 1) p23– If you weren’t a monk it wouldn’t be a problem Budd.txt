Celibacy is the rule for all, whether a novice or fully ordained.  For Buddhist lay people, the standard is to ‘refrain from sexual misconduct’, so sexual relations between consenting adults, where no one is being taken advantage of or deceived, are considered completely appropriate.   2) ibid:– when he was questioned about his views: This dialogue between the Buddha and Ariṭṭha is found at M 22 and it is also the basis of Pac #68 (Vin iv 134). This kind of inappropriate indulgence is also mentioned in the origin story and text of Saṅg #4, (Vin iv 1-2); this was laid down based on the actions of Bhikkhu Udāyin. ‘Should any bhikkhu, lustful, with perverted thoughts, speak in a woman’s presence in praise of ministering to his own sensuality, inviting sexual intercourse thus, “Sister, the highest kind of ministering is this, namely, ministering with this (sexual) act to a virtuous lofty-natured observer of the Brahmacariya such as I am”, this entails initial and subsequent meeting of the Sangha,’ (Bhikkhu Ñāṇamoli trans.).  3) p24:– keep remembering ‘Sooner! More!’: As in Mara and the Mangala I, Ch. 15, p179.  4) ibid:– like the Holy Deva Dharani who rose up as a witness: One of the most popular legends around the Buddha’s enlightenment was that, when Māra’s army would not acknowledge defeat and Māra called his followers to be his witnesses to his claim to supremacy in the universe, the Bodhisattva reached down and touched the earth, to call the Earth Mother, Dharani, to be his witness and to vouch for his genuine supremacy. A version of this story is found in many late accounts of the Buddha’s life, such as the Buddhavaṃsa and the Lalitavisatara. A very readable account is found in Oriental Mythology, by Joseph Campbell, pp21–22.   5) ibid:– Didn’t he have a deva friend too?: As found at S 1.20  6) p25:– so they would have to squeeze by you: This incident involved Udāyin and was the basis for Saṅg #2 and #3, (Vin iii 119 and Vin iii 127).  7) ibid:– but Udayin too: As above, Udāyin was the cause of the establishment of numerous Vinaya rules, see for example Dictionary of Pali Proper Names, p376, §2.  8) ibid:– that is when the self realizes the highest Nirvana: This passage is found at D 1.3.20.

20)        ibid:– you can go in there and think it’s just a day: This structure of things is informed not just by the traditional cosmology of Buddhism but also by many European and other Asian legends and fairy tales. For example the story of Oisin, being taken to the ‘Land of Youth’, Tir na N’og, by its queen, where ‘what he thought was seven days was three times thrice seven years back in County Clare.’ Also for example, the story of King Herla, as recounted by Walter Map in the late 12th Century, in his book De Nugis Curialium. Chapter 5: The Uninspiring Monk  1) p31:– addressing Udayin about how misguided he had been: This incident formed the basis for the Saṅghadisesa rule #2 and similar incidents with Ven. Udāyin were the basis for Saṅg. #3 as well. Needless to say, Krishna’s presence in the scenario is an addition by the author.   2)         ibid:– and then to let go of attachment to the pleasure we feel: This is the opposite of what is stated by the Buddha at M 22.9. With respect to the idea of experiencing sense pleasure without attachment he says: ‘Bhikkhus, that one can engage in sensual pleasures [e.g. sexual intercourse] without sensual desires, without perceptions of sensual desire, without thoughts of sensual desire – that is impossible,’ (Bhikkhu Ñāṇamoli & Bhikkhu Bodhi trans.).  3)         ibid:– the scanty virtue and wisdom of the narrow-minded: This phrase is borrowed from the Vimalakirti Nirdesa Sūtra, where a ‘superior’ view to that of the perceived-as-small-minded Śrāvaka is espoused. For example:   Then Vimalakirti told Śāriputra and all the other Śrāvakas, ‘Venerables, you may come and eat of the food of the Tathāgata. It is ambrosia infused with great compassion. Do not eat it with a narrow mind for if you do so you will not be able to digest it.’     But some Śrāvakas thought, ‘How can such a small amount of food feed everyone in this great assembly?’

  The transformation-bodied Bodhisattva said, ‘Do not use the scanty virtue and wisdom of a Śrāvaka to measure the immeasurable merit and wisdom of the Tathāgata! The four oceans might dry up but this food cannot be exhausted.’  Vimalakirti Nirdesa Sūtra 10.104  4)         p32:– how he led his half-brother Nanda to full enlightenment: The story is found at Ud 3.2.  5)         ibid:– crowds of apsaras, nymphs with feet as delicate as doves: The Pali term for these beings is accharā kakuṭapāda. ‘Apsarā’ is the Skt for ‘accharā’. They are celestial musicians and dancers who abide in the Tavatiṃsa Heaven. See e.g. S 1.46, Jāt 182, as well as Ud 3.2.  6)          ibid:– as soft as the breasts of doves: This phrase is borrowed from the poem To a Lady Seen From the Train, by Frances Cornford.   7)          p33:– thoroughly convincing examples he gave: This is an echo, or more accurately a mirror image, of the advice given to Krishna’s father, Kāmanīta, in Ch. 20 of The Pilgrim Kāmanīta – ‘The Unreasonable Child’.   8)          ibid:– that the old seeress Gavinna had spoken of: This is a reference to Mara and the Mangala I, Ch. 26, p335.   9)          p34:– a substantial group of monks walking with him: This was the usual way that the Buddha travelled – always on foot and accompanied by a large group of monks. From time to time he walked with just one attendant monk, such as Ud 8.7, and very rarely, all alone, such as at M 128.5-6.  10)       ibid:– any negative actions by body speech or mind: This ceremony is regularly practised in Southern Buddhist countries. It is carried out when one is about to depart from a monastery, at the end of a time when one has been living close to others for an extended period, or when it will be useful to ‘clean the slate’ and to acknowledge any hurt that might have been caused.   11)         ibid:– apprenticed to him, in ‘dependence’: A monk or a nun is expected to ‘take dependence on a teacher’ (‘acariya’ in Pali), for at least the %rst %ve years of their monastic training. This is so that they have a responsible mentor and teacher for this formative period, just as a seamstress, a baker, a carpenter, a riding instructor etc. would be apprenticed to a mentor for, say, %ve to seven years to learn their craft.

12)         p35:– should not be getting ahead of the Elder: A standard that is maintained in strict monasteries, especially of the Southern Tradition, is that when walking together the senior always walks in front.   13)         ibid:– Assaji and Punabbasuka: These two badly behaved but in"uential monks resided at Kīṭāgiri, seemingly for a long time. They are mentioned as the cause of Saṅg #13, where the story is related much as recounted here. It is found at Vin. iii 179-82. The Buddha comes to give them advice as recounted in the Kīṭāgiri Sutta, M 70.   The two Elders were also known as members of the notorious ‘Group of Six’ (chabbaggiya) monks who were responsible for the laying down of numerous training precepts by the Buddha, on account of their unruly behaviour and attitudes. The other members were Mettiya and Bhummajaka, who were based in Rājagaha, and Panduka and Lohitaka, who resided at the Jetavana.   NB This ‘Assaji’ is not to be confused with ‘the enlightened Elder Assaji’ who was one of the original %ve disciples of the Buddha. They are very di#erent people but, ironically, they shared the same name.  14)        ibid:– a pattern of yantras: ‘Yantra’ is a Skt word for a symbolic pattern or matrix of letters, numbers and symbols, used since ancient times in India and South-East Asia to act as a protective charm or to bestow particular powers or enchantments. Yantras are also used as meditation objects or symbols of spiritual truths.   The game referred to in this passage of the story is translated by I.B. Horner as ‘they played a game of stepping onto diagrams’.  15)       p36:– his bowl was half-concealed by his robe: This is one of the stipulations of the Vinaya for monks and nuns going on alms round in villages and towns.   16)       p37:– he was walking at an even pace: Similarly, monastics are encouraged to walk ‘neither too fast or too slow’ when on alms-round. Likewise, in the Brahmāyu Sutta (M 91.10), it describes how the Buddha ‘walks neither too quickly or too slowly’.   17)      ibid:– those who go about in his name are not following his True Path: Again, this story is all based directly on the origin of Saṅg. #13, although neither the monk or the faithful lay-disciple are named in the Vinaya text, (see Vin iii 179-82).
